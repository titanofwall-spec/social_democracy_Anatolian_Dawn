# Cyprus source passages: 15–20 July 1974
Six dated events contain translations of selected passages, not newly composed narrative. Separate excerpts are separated by paragraph breaks. Original mod scenes and Atilla I decisions are preserved.
Source: Mehmet Ali Birand, 30 Sıcak Gün, supplied file 83647000-30-sicak-gun.pdf. PDF page numbers are one-based physical pages; printed page numbers are the book footers.
Diyet was inspected for scope and relevant names. Its introduction (PDF page 6) and contents (PDF page 12) describe the period after the Cyprus operation and open with Geneva II; it was not used as a source for these pre-operation scenes.
Editorial treatment: line wraps and broken words are joined in translation, evident OCR errors corrected, footnote markers omitted from the displayed prose. Original extracted Turkish is retained below, including OCR defects. Dialogue is Birand's reported/reconstructed account, not claimed to be a verbatim official transcript. Source notes on PDF pages 71, 110, 112 and 122 describe interviews, publications, and eyewitness accounts; page 43 refers to Appendix 1.
Chronology: the first appeals for calm appear under 15 July, not 16 July. The 15 July account includes the cabinet meeting continuing until 02:30 on 16 July. The 19 July chapter includes Sisco's overnight Ankara meeting at 01:45 on 20 July (PDF pages 121–122). The opening speech on PDF page 130 concludes that overnight chapter, but explicitly concerns the operation starting on 20 July. Existing fictionalized scenes keep their existing dates and wording; their chronology is not rewritten here.

English translation:

Immediately after news of the coup, the eyes of the whole world turned to Ankara. Messages began pouring into the Foreign Ministry by telegram, telephone and visits from ambassadors. Led by UN Secretary-General Waldheim and NATO Secretary-General Luns, Britain, America, France and all the allies repeated the scenario familiar from years past: "Please act calmly."

English translation:

Ecevit concluded the discussions as follows:

"To invoke Article 4 of the Treaty of Guarantee, drawn up for the independence of Cyprus, and to change the Sampson regime, a Turkish military presence on the island is necessary. In this way, we can protect both Turkey's security and that of the Turkish Cypriots. Preparations must begin. Meanwhile, we shall also contact Britain, the other guarantor country, and try to achieve our objectives without bloodshed. If we obtain no result from Britain, we shall have completed our preparations to exercise our right in practice."

English translation:

That night, Ecevit told his ministers that Greece could no longer be regarded as a guarantor country and that he would therefore hold talks with Britain. Even those who understood that intervention lay at the end of the road had countless questions. What would the Greek reaction be? What if a Turkish-Greek war broke out? And so on, and so on.

At around 2.30 in the morning, the ministers gave the prime minister "full authority". This authority covered conducting diplomatic contacts with Britain and other countries, as well as following the recommendations of the National Security Council.

English translation:

Immediately after Ecevit repeated what he had said to Wilson and Callaghan, Sisco took the floor and began speaking quite forcefully to dissuade Turkey. He had also brought a message from Kissinger to the same effect:

"America opposes a Turkish intervention because of the coup. We think this could lead to war between two NATO members."

After stating that Turkey had no intention of going to war with Greece, Ecevit tried to dispel the American administration's concerns. Beginning with the decision on poppy cultivation, he discussed Turkish-American relations and the periods through which they had passed. He even said that friendships could endure as long as they rested on sound and equal foundations. He said that even the Sixth Fleet could now visit Turkish ports more safely, because relations rested on a healthy foundation:

"...But it is impossible for Turkey to turn a blind eye to a fait accompli in Cyprus."

"But you are not considering Greece's reaction. They will not leave this unanswered. War will break out and the region will be thrown into turmoil. You should know that your relations with the United States will also deteriorate."

"That is for them to decide."
